VEGETABLE NUTRITION REFERENCE
Nutritional facts per 100 g of edible portion
========================================================================

This long text document contains approximate nutritional information for
commonly consumed vegetables. It is designed both as a general reference
and as a test document for Python text parsing, extraction, searching,
chunking, indexing, and natural-language-processing workflows.

Unless otherwise stated, values refer to 100 g of the raw edible portion.
Nutrition can vary with variety, maturity, growing conditions, storage,
cooking method, and the nutritional database used.

FIELDS USED THROUGHOUT THIS DOCUMENT:
Calories (kcal), Carbohydrates (g), Protein (g), Fat (g),
Dietary Fiber (g), Sugars (g), and Water (g).

NOTE: Botanically, some foods commonly called vegetables are fruits.
Examples include tomato, cucumber, pumpkin, eggplant, and bell pepper.
Here they are included because they are commonly classified or used as
vegetables in everyday cooking.

========================================================================
LOW-CALORIE VEGETABLE EXAMPLES
========================================================================

Very low-calorie examples in this reference include cucumber, lettuce,
bottle gourd, celery, radish, bok choy, tomato, zucchini, and asparagus.

These foods contain a high proportion of water and can add volume to meals
while contributing relatively few calories per 100 g.

========================================================================
HIGHER-CALORIE VEGETABLE EXAMPLES
========================================================================

Some foods in the vegetable category contain considerably more calories.
Examples include garlic, taro root, yam, corn, potato, and sweet potato.

Root and tuber vegetables often contain more starch and carbohydrate than
leafy vegetables. This does not make them unhealthy; they simply have a
different nutritional profile and energy density.

========================================================================
FIBER-RICH VEGETABLE EXAMPLES
========================================================================

Relatively high-fiber examples include kale, Brussels sprouts, green peas,
yam, taro root, bitter gourd, okra, eggplant, and sweet potato.

Fiber is a type of carbohydrate that is not completely digested by the
human digestive system. It contributes to normal digestive function and
can be part of a balanced dietary pattern.

========================================================================
RAW VERSUS COOKED VEGETABLES
========================================================================

Cooking can change the weight and nutrient concentration of vegetables.
Boiling may cause some water-soluble nutrients to move into cooking water.
Steaming can preserve the structure of many vegetables while softening them.
Roasting can reduce water content and therefore increase nutrient values
per 100 g of the cooked food because the cooked food weighs less.

Therefore, raw and cooked nutrition values should not always be compared
directly by weight.

1. All nutritional numbers are approximate.
2. Values are presented per 100 g of edible portion.

7. This document is a general reference and not individualized medical advice.

========================================================================
EXAMPLE COMPARISON
========================================================================

Consider three common vegetables:

Spinach: 23 kcal, 2.9 g protein, 2.2 g fiber per 100 g.
Broccoli: 34 kcal, 2.8 g protein, 2.6 g fiber per 100 g.
Potato: 77 kcal, 2.0 g protein, 2.2 g fiber per 100 g.

These examples demonstrate how vegetables can differ substantially in
calorie density and macronutrient composition.

Leafy vegetables often contain a large amount of water and relatively few
calories. Starchy roots and tubers generally provide more carbohydrate.
